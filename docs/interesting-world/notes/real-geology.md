# Real geology for cliffs, rock structures and caves

Research notes for OdinCraft v5, 2026-11-11. They extend `reference/odincraft-v4/explorable-places-design.md` and `exploration-diversity-research.md`. Those notes cover how to *assemble* a place (place graph, sparse 3D modifiers, supplied falls). These notes cover *where real geology puts such places* and what they measure. Sources are linked inline. Abstracts were often all that could be reached; "unverified" marks numbers that came only from secondary pages.

## Key takeaways

1. **Rock structure decides where landforms go.** A few parameters per rock unit explain nearly every cliff, ledge, fin, arch, sea cave and waterfall: thickness, strength, dip and strike, joint spacing, and permeability/solubility. Selby's rock-mass-strength scheme uses seven factors (intact strength, weathering, joint spacing, orientation, width, continuity/infill, groundwater) ([Selby 1982, ESP&L](https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/esp.3290070506); [UNT course notes](https://sites.geography.unt.edu/~williams/geog_3350/examreviews/strength_of_rock.htm)).
2. **A cliff is a strong layer over a weaker one, or ground cut faster than slopes can lay back.** Waterfalls are steep where the top rock is stronger than the rock below ([NPS Delaware Water Gap](https://www.nps.gov/dewa/learn/nature/waterfalls.htm)). Coast cliffs are steep where resistant rock meets high wave energy ([NPS rocky coasts](https://home.nps.gov/articles/rocky-coast-landforms.htm)).
3. **Soil-mantled slopes stop near 30°.** Above a mean slope of about 30°, slopes no longer steepen as erosion speeds up; rock exposure rises sharply instead ([DiBiase et al. 2012](https://www.public.asu.edu/~aheimsat/publications/DiBiase%20et%20al%20ESPL%2012.pdf); [Burbank et al. 1996](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996Natur.379..505B/abstract); [Binnie et al. 2007](https://www.waterboards.ca.gov/waterrights/water_issues/programs/enforcement/complaints/docs/investigation/1_binnie_2007_geology_preprint.pdf)). Talus sits at about 33–35°, within a 30–40° range ([Francou & Manté 1990](https://horizon.documentation.ird.fr/exl-doc/pleins_textes/divers19-08/010048761.pdf); [WOU notes](https://people.wou.edu/~taylors/g322/masswast.pdf)). For the generator: soil below ~30°, scree at 33–35°, bare rock only above that.
4. **Joints set the grain size of everything.** Joint spacing sets column, fin and block widths, and decides tor against regolith. Columnar joints in basalt are about 0.4–0.5 m across at the Giant's Causeway ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/EBchecked/topic/233107/Giants-Causeway)), and column width scales inversely with cooling rate ([Goehring & Morris 2008](https://www.physics.utoronto.ca/nonlinear/abstracts/GM_JGR08abstract.html)). All of these are smaller than a 16 m cell, so the simulation should carry joint *spacing and orientation*, and voxels should draw them.
5. **Waterfalls cluster at a threshold drainage area.** In New Zealand's Waipaoa basin, about 70% of 236 waterfalls sit where 0.1–1 km² drains past them ([Crosby & Whipple 2006](https://core.ac.uk/download/4403051.pdf)). On a 6 km island, small tributaries hanging over a trunk valley or sea cliff are the natural waterfall host.
6. **Caves need a soluble or lava host.** Solution caves form only in limestone, dolomite, marble, gypsum or salt, along joints, faults and bedding ([NPS solution caves](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/caves/solution-caves.htm)). Lava tubes need pāhoehoe-style basalt ([NPS Hawaiʻi Volcanoes](https://www.nps.gov/havo/learn/nature/lava-tubes.htm)). Anywhere else, realistic caves are sea caves, talus caves, fracture caves and rock shelters, which are small and near the surface.
7. **Coastal landforms follow the weak lines in the rock.** Sea caves, geos, arches and stacks form where waves exploit joints, faults, dikes and soft beds on exposed headlands ([NPS sea caves](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/caves/sea-or-littoral-caves.htm); [St Abb's Head GCR](https://geoguide.scottishgeologytrust.org/page/2386)). Coves form where the sea breaches a hard band and reaches soft rock behind it ([Geological Society, Lulworth](https://www.geolsoc.org.uk/GeositesLulworth)).
8. **Landslides make lakes and big scarps.** A resistant cap over weak rock fails in large slides: at Trotternish, basalt over Jurassic mudstone gives the largest landslide terrain in Britain, nearly 40 km² ([Scottish Geology Trust](https://geoguide.scottishgeologytrust.org/page/2842); [AGU landslide blog](https://blogs.agu.org/landslideblog/2021/01/04/trotternish-landslide-complex/)). Juniko's 33 lakes sit in landslide debris ([Ministry of Environment Japan](https://www.env.go.jp/nature/isan/worldheritage/en/shirakami/area/a06.html); [Hirosaki Univ. leaflet](https://nature.hirosaki-u.ac.jp/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/A4_leaflet.pdf)). Most landslide dams fail early: about half within 10 days in Costa & Schuster's 73 cases ([USGS](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70013832)). Lakes that last sit behind dams that are wide, coarse, or have seepage outlets.
9. **Glacial forms need a past glaciation.** A temperate island with peaks of a few hundred metres has cirques only at high latitude (Faroes, Scotland). Without that history they would break the "realism comes from causes" rule. Volcanic forms only need a young volcanic history.
10. **Use a two-scale split.** The 16 m simulation should know about rock units, dip, joint orientation, cliff-forming contacts, knickpoints, landslide scars and coast exposure. Voxels should draw undercuts, columns, notches, caves, arches and talus blocks.

## 1. Rock structure as the hidden variable

**Lithology and strength.** Rock strength governs slope steepness through rock mass strength, not intact strength alone. Joints dipping into a slope count as stronger than joints dipping out of it ([UNT notes on Selby](https://sites.geography.unt.edu/~williams/geog_3350/examreviews/strength_of_rock.htm)). Incision rate in one field study scaled with the square of rock tensile strength ([Stock et al. 2005, USGS](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70029267)). Generator rule: erodibility K ∝ 1/strength², which means a few strength classes produce a large contrast in erosion.

**Bedding and dip.**
- *Flat-lying layers* give mesas, benches and stepped valley sides. In the Faroes, a staircase follows basalt flows averaging about 20 m thick with thin tuff interbeds, and the steps nearly vanish where flows are thin ([Trap Faroe Islands](https://trap.fo/en/nature-and-landscape/geologi-pa-faeroerne/); [Pitts 1981](https://bcgs.info/pub/wp-content/uploads/pdf_files/Pitts_1981_Geology_of_the_Faroe_Islands.pdf)). Rule: a step appears per resistant unit thicker than about one step height. Steps are cliffs where the unit is strong and soil slopes where it is weak.
- *Gentle dip (below about 20°)* gives a cuesta: a scarp on the up-dip side and a dip slope that follows the resistant bed. Mesa Verde dips about 7°, and its alcoves form where water perched on a shale seeps out along the dip ([NPS Mesa Verde](https://www.nps.gov/meve/learn/nature/geology.htm); [MIT notes](https://web.mit.edu/oeit/pgt/index-part=6.3.0.html)).
- *Steep dip* gives hogbacks, with both flanks of similar steepness, and flatirons, which are triangular facets of a steep resistant bed. The angle cut-offs between these forms are arbitrary in the literature (pointer: [Wikipedia, hogback](https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/Hogback_(geology))).

**Joints.** The overlying rock at Arches cracked into "a series of more-or-less parallel lines" over a rising salt dome. Fins stand between the joints, and arches open where water and frost weaken a lower layer. The park has over 2,000 arches with spans up to 97 m in an 18–23 cm/yr rainfall climate ([NPS Arches](https://www.nps.gov/arch/learn/nature/arches.htm)). Fin width equals joint spacing; I could not find an authoritative measured value. Columnar joints form by cooling contraction. Columns are 38–51 cm across at the Giant's Causeway, about 40,000 of them, mostly five- to seven-sided ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/EBchecked/topic/233107/Giants-Causeway)). A flow typically shows a lower colonnade of regular columns, an entablature of chaotic thin jointing, and an upper colonnade. Staffa stacks these on a tuff base ([Phillips et al. 2013, Durham repository](https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/output/1470309)). Faster cooling gives thinner columns ([Goehring & Morris 2008](https://www.physics.utoronto.ca/nonlinear/abstracts/GM_JGR08abstract.html)). Welded tuff also forms columns; the v4 notes cover Takachiho. Devils Postpile diameters could not be found on an NPS page. The figures I found (0.3–0.6 m) are unverified.

**Dikes and sills.** At Spanish Peaks, dikes range from about 0.3 m to over 30 m wide (most are 1–3 m) and run up to about 22 km. Where they resist erosion better than the sediments they cut, they stand as near-vertical walls up to about 30 m high. Some mafic dikes weather faster than the rock around them and form trenches instead ([USGS PP 594-G](https://pubs.usgs.gov/pp/0594g/report.pdf)). On a coast, the same rule makes a dike either a skerry line and platform ridge (Dunbar) or a slot. Arran's Kildonan shore has whole dike swarms ([Scottish Geology Trust GCR](https://geoguide.scottishgeologytrust.org/page/2386); [Arran geology](https://www.arranmuseum.co.uk/what-will-you-see/geology/the-geological-history)). A sill gives a ledge or waterfall lip. At Trotternish, a dolerite sill defines the base of the slide surface ([Scottish Geology Trust](https://geoguide.scottishgeologytrust.org/page/2842)).

**Caprock over weak rock.** Weaker layers recede and stronger layers jut out ([NPS Bryce hoodoos](https://www.nps.gov/brca/learn/nature/hoodoos.htm)). This undercutting gives alcoves, overhangs, waterfalls that retreat by collapse, and, at scale, rotational landslides.

**Faults.** A fault puts different rocks side by side, guides erosion along its crushed zone, and can leave a scarp. Arran's Highland Boundary Fault splits the island into a northern granite highland and a southern sedimentary lowland ([Arran Museum](https://www.arranmuseum.co.uk/what-will-you-see/geology/the-geological-history)). At St Abb's Head, minor faults at right angles to a major fault become geos and inlets ([GCR](https://geoguide.scottishgeologytrust.org/page/2386)). I found no authoritative source for fault-scarp dimensions at island scale.

**Minimal parameters for a rock body (my inference).** Per unit: top and bottom surfaces, or thickness plus a regional dip vector; strength class; joint sets (one to three), each with strike, dip and spacing; permeability; solubility (carbonate or not); and a volcanic flag for columnar or flow structure. Intrusions are planar bodies with a width and a strength contrast.

## 2. Cliffs and slopes

- **Threshold slopes.** River incision of 2–12 mm/yr in the Himalaya leaves hillslope angles nearly independent of erosion rate, because landsliding keeps pace with the rivers ([Burbank et al. 1996](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996Natur.379..505B/abstract)). Soil transport rises nonlinearly toward a critical gradient, so soil-mantled slopes are convex at the crest and planar below it ([Roering et al. 1999](https://zendy.io/title/10.1029/1998wr900090)). Rule: a slope is soil-mantled if soil production can keep up. Where the river lowers the base faster, the slope becomes threshold-limited (about 30–35°) and loses its soil. Cliffs need strength above what threshold landsliding allows, which means massive or favourably jointed rock (Selby's strength-equilibrium slopes, [ESP&L 1982](https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/esp.3290070506)).
- **Cliff profile.** From the top down: a soil-mantled convex crest, the free face (its height equals the thickness of the strong unit, or the incision depth into massive rock), then a talus apron at 33–35° ([Francou & Manté](https://horizon.documentation.ird.fr/exl-doc/pleins_textes/divers19-08/010048761.pdf)). Talus is near repose only while supply is active or its toe is being removed (pointer: [Wikipedia, talus](https://wikiwand.com/en/Talus_slope)).
- **V-valley, gorge or slot.** Bedrock channel width depends on discharge and on slope. Steeper, faster-incising reaches are narrower than discharge alone predicts ([Finnegan et al. 2005](https://earthweb.ess.washington.edu/roe/Web/GerardWeb/Publications_files/FinneganEtal_Width_Geology05.pdf)). My inference for a rule: a gorge forms where incision outpaces wall lay-back. That happens in strong, massive or vertically jointed rock just downstream of a migrating knickpoint. Weak rock gives a V-valley at threshold angle. A slot needs strong rock, a small catchment and fast incision. I found no authoritative numeric threshold.
- **Knickpoints and waterfalls.** These come from base-level fall (the island's sea-level history, a trunk river cutting down, landslide dams) or from a strength contrast in the bed. At Waipaoa, about 70% of knickpoints sit at 10⁵–10⁶ m² drainage area, and more than half sit within 1 km of a large tributary junction ([Crosby & Whipple 2006](https://core.ac.uk/download/4403051.pdf)). Falls in rock with vertical and horizontal joints keep a vertical face by toppling blocks; plunge-pool depth controls this ([Lamb & Dietrich 2009](https://lamb.caltech.edu/documents/19602/Lamb_Dietrich_GSA_2009.pdf)). Amphitheatre heads form in columnar basalt. Example height: Senpiro-no-taki on Yakushima falls 60 m in granite ([MLIT](https://www.mlit.go.jp/tagengo-db/common/001549548.docx)). I found no source for typical waterfall height by stream size.
- **Terraces.** Strath terraces form when a river widens its bed in bedrock and then incises again ([Collins et al. 2016](https://faculty.washington.edu/bcollins/publications_pdfs/Collins_et_al_2016_Rates_and_mechanisms_of_bedrock_incision_and_strath_terrace_formation_in_a_forested_catchment_Cascade_Range_Washington.pdf)). On an island, base-level fall gives flights of terraces. Sea-level rise does the opposite: it gives drowned mouths with aggrading floors.
- **Landslides and dammed lakes.** Large slides need a weak layer under load, a slope undercut by a river or the sea, and a trigger such as an earthquake or heavy rain. Trotternish has rotated, translated and toppled blocks below a basalt scarp, with up to 300 m of sediment under a similar thickness of basalt ([Scottish Geology Trust](https://geoguide.scottishgeologytrust.org/page/2842)). Juniko: about 33 lakes over about 4 km² in the debris and depressions of a slide traditionally tied to the 1704 earthquake. Research suggests an older slide made most of the lakes and the 1704 event enlarged the scarp ([Ministry of Environment Japan](https://www.env.go.jp/nature/isan/worldheritage/en/shirakami/area/a06.html); [Hirosaki leaflet](https://nature.hirosaki-u.ac.jp/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/A4_leaflet.pdf); [JpGU 2022](https://confit.atlas.jp/guide/event/jpgu2022/subject/HDS07-P02/date)). Aoike's blue water is fed by groundwater. That is a sign of a seepage outlet through the debris rather than a surface spillway. Generator consequence: a slide produces a scarp (a fresh cliff), hummocky debris with closed hollows that become ponds, and a dammed upstream lake whose outlet seeps or cuts a gorge.

## 3. Residual and weathering forms

- **Tors and corestones.** Granite weathers deeply along closely spaced joints, leaving corestones where joints are widely spaced. Stripping the regolith exposes them as tors on summits and valley shoulders ([Dartmoor NPA factsheet](https://geographysouthwest.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2020/08/DNPA-Factsheet-Tors.pdf); [Scottish Geology Trust](https://geoguide.scottishgeologytrust.org/page/1057)). Dartmoor tors are mostly under 100 ka in exposure age (Gunnell et al. 2013, cited in [IUGS](https://iugs-geoheritage.org/geoheritage_sites/granite-landforms-of-dartmoor/)). Yakushima has corestone boulder fields ([JSEG](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jjseg1960/48/2/48_2_90/_article/-char/en)). Up close: stacked rounded blocks a few metres across, with horizontal joints, sitting on a field of loose blocks (clitter).
- **Exfoliation domes.** Sheeting joints run parallel to the surface of massive granite ([Physics Today on Half Dome](https://physicstoday.aip.org/news/explaining-sheeting-joints-on-yosemites-half-dome); [USGS Yosemite story](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/eot2008/20081109090055/http://wrgis.wr.usgs.gov/docs/parks/yos/topobk.html)). I could not verify sheet thicknesses.
- **Tafoni and honeycomb weathering.** Salt-driven cavities form in sandstone and granite in the spray zone and the intertidal zone. Honeycomb cells are under about 2 cm; tafoni are larger and grade up to small shelters ([ESP&L 2010 abstract, via pointer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honeycomb_weathering)). These are a texture or small voxel feature on coastal rock.
- **Hoodoos.** Calcareous, layered rock in a climate with over 170 freeze–thaw nights a year breaks down from plateau to wall to window to hoodoo ([NPS Bryce](https://www.nps.gov/brca/learn/nature/hoodoos.htm)). This is unlikely on a mild maritime island; earth pillars under boulder caps in till are a likelier analogue (not researched).
- **Arches.** Inland arches need jointed fins and a weak or porous lower layer ([NPS Arches](https://www.nps.gov/arch/learn/nature/arches.htm)). Sea arches are covered in section 4.
- **Rock shelters.** These form where a weak or seeping layer recedes under a strong one (the Mesa Verde alcoves, [NPS](https://www.nps.gov/meve/learn/nature/geology.htm)). They occur along the base of every caprock cliff, and above all at a seepage contact.

## 4. Coasts

- **Cliffs against beaches.** Resistant rock under high wave energy makes a steep profile. Weak rock under low energy makes a gentle one. Massive rock erodes uniformly and layered rock erodes in steps. Waves refract onto headlands and concentrate erosion there ([NPS rocky coasts](https://home.nps.gov/articles/rocky-coast-landforms.htm)). Rule: cliffs face the dominant swell and fetch, and beaches, spits and dunes sit on the lee side and in bays.
- **Platforms and notches.** Platforms widen with wave intensity and are widest facing the dominant waves. They narrow as rock dip or cliff height increases ([Trenhaile, J. Coastal Research](https://journals.flvc.org/jcr/article/view/80508)). Notch height correlates with wave exposure. Notch backwearing is below 2 mm/yr in one study ([Geologica Acta 2015](https://ddd.uab.cat/pub/geoact/geoact_a2015m6v13n2/geoact_a2015m6v13n2p167.pdf)). Weathering, not only waves, cuts many platforms.
- **Sea caves, geos, arches, stacks.** Waves enlarge joints, faults, dikes, veins and soft beds. Caves along faults are long, narrow and straight. Painted Cave on Santa Cruz Island runs 374 m ([NPS sea caves](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/caves/sea-or-littoral-caves.htm); [UC Davis](https://cwseducation.ucdavis.edu/class/56/faults-basalts-sea-caves-and-sea-stacks-santa-cruz-island)). Caves form in weaker rock on exposed promontories. Arches collapse into stacks, and none of these forms is permanent ([NPS rocky coasts](https://home.nps.gov/articles/rocky-coast-landforms.htm)). Sequence along one joint: notch, cave, blowhole or geo through roof collapse; at a headland, two caves meet to form an arch, then a stack, then a stump.
- **Coves and headlands.** At Lulworth, the sea breached a Portland limestone barrier, hollowed out the soft Purbeck, Wealden and Greensand behind it, and stopped against the chalk. Stair Hole next door is a cove just beginning ([Geological Society](https://www.geolsoc.org.uk/GeositesLulworth)). Rule: bands parallel to the shore make a single breach that becomes an oval cove. Bands at right angles to the shore make alternating headlands and bays.
- **Raised beaches.** These need land uplift faster than sea-level rise. In Jura they are gravel ridges up to about 40 m above sea level, with 31 ridges recording about 12 m of uplift over 6,500 years ([Scottish Geology Trust GCR, west Jura](https://geoguide.scottishgeologytrust.org/p/gcr/gcr06/gcr06_westcoastofjura); [BGS Earthwise](https://earthwise.bgs.ac.uk/index.php/Raised_beaches,_Pleistocene_and_Recent,_Northern_Highlands_of_Scotland)). OdinCraft's history (a sea-level *rise* drowning the valley mouths) produces rias instead. Raised beaches would contradict it unless the island also rose; this is a choice for the owner.
- **Rias, tombolos, spits, dunes, tidal flats.** These are well-known depositional forms that I did not research further here. They need sediment supply from rivers or eroding cliffs and a sheltered side.

## 5. Caves

- **Solution caves.** Rainwater made acidic by soil CO₂ dissolves rock along joints, faults and bedding. Cave streams leave at springs, and the drop from inlet to spring drives development; the steepest case is a vertical pit. Active passages grow at most about 1 mm/yr, so a passage reaches about 1 m in 1,000 years and about 2 m in 2,000. Sinkholes range from a few feet to miles across and drain into passages below ([NPS solution caves](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/caves/solution-caves.htm)). Caves take 10⁴–10⁵ years to reach a size a person can pass through ([Palmer 1991 abstract](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/kip_articles/4015)).
- **Patterns (Palmer 1991).** Point recharge from sinkholes and sinking streams gives *branchwork*: tributaries join downstream like a surface river network. Diffuse recharge or flooding gives *network mazes* in fractured rock, *anastomotic mazes* along low-angle partings, or *spongework*. Rising thermal water gives *ramiform* caves. Structure orients passages but does not decide branchwork against maze ([Palmer abstract](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/kip_articles/4015); [GW-Project karst book](https://books.gw-project.org/introduction-to-karst-aquifers/?p=365)). Insoluble beds (chert, shale) confine passages to particular horizons. Multilevel caves form where the river base level drops in stages (same source).
- **Passage shapes.** Phreatic passages are tubes, round or lens-shaped in section along bedding. Vadose passages are canyons with shafts on joints. A keyhole passage is a phreatic tube with a vadose trench cut into its floor after the water table fell ([GW-Project](https://books.gw-project.org/introduction-to-karst-aquifers/?p=365); [Earle, Environmental Geology](https://geo.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Geology/Environmental_Geology_(Earle)/12%3A_Karst_and_Caves/12.04%3A_Karst_Cave_Features_Cave_Contents_and_Subterranean_Life)). I could not find authoritative typical passage dimensions; the NPS growth rate above gives metre-scale passages.
- **Entrances (my inference from the sources above).** Entrances are where the cave meets the surface: a sinking stream at the upstream contact where a river crosses onto limestone; a spring or resurgence at the lowest outcrop of limestone next to the base level; collapse dolines; and a valley wall that cuts across an old, higher passage level. Density: sinkhole plains reach 5–8 sinkholes per km² ([Veni 2002](https://legacy.caves.org/pub/journal/PDF/V64/v64n1-Veni.pdf)), and doline karst reaches tens to hundreds per km² ([Britannica](https://britannica.com/science/cave/Karst-topography)). I found no reliable cave-entrance density for comparing karst with non-karst ground.
- **Lava tubes.** A lava channel crusts over, the insulated flow cuts down by thermal erosion, and the tube drains when the eruption stops. Roof collapses make skylights ([NPS Hawaiʻi Volcanoes](https://www.nps.gov/havo/learn/nature/lava-tubes.htm)). Kīlauea tubes are typically about 5 m high and 3 m wide; tubes in inflated sheet flows are 1–2 m high and tens of metres wide ([USGS 1997](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70020452)). Jeju's Manjanggul is about 7.4 km long, with sections of roughly 18–23 m wide and up to about 30 m high (unverified, secondary sources) ([UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/document/152211)). Tubes run downslope from a vent, just under the flow surface.
- **Other caves.** Sea caves are covered in section 4. Talus caves form as voids among large blocks under cliffs or in landslide debris. Fracture caves are open joints behind landslide blocks; Trotternish's toppled blocks are the host. Rock shelters are covered in section 3.

## 6. Volcanic and glacial forms

- **Cinder cones.** A fresh cone's height is about 0.18 of its base diameter, and its crater about 0.40 of the base ([Porter 1972 via Wood 1980](https://people.wou.edu/~taylors/g407/wood_1980.pdf)). Height ratios fall to about 0.08 as cones degrade, while the crater ratio stays constant. Jeju has about 360 such cones (oreum) (unverified count, [UNESCO nomination copy](https://www.thesalmons.org/lynn/wh-wcmc/Korea-Jeju.pdf)). A 600 m cone stands about 100 m high. These need basaltic volcanism younger than about 1 Ma to keep their shape (my inference from the degradation trend).
- **Plugs, necks, calderas.** Ball's Pyramid is the eroded plug of a shield volcano. Mount Gower and Lidgbird are caldera-filling lavas that resisted erosion, so the old caldera now stands highest ([LHI Museum](https://lhimuseum.com/learn/geology/)). That is an inverted relief rule: a strong infill outlasts the weaker cone around it.
- **Glacial forms.** Measured cirques average 0.5–1 km in length and width, favour north to north-east aspects, and have floors that track former snowlines ([Iceland and Kamchatka studies](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9603333/)). Glaciers cut the Faroes' stepped valley sides into the basalt ([Pitts 1981](https://bcgs.info/pub/wp-content/uploads/pdf_files/Pitts_1981_Geology_of_the_Faroe_Islands.pdf)). Cirques, tarns, U-shaped valleys, hanging valleys, roches moutonnées, erratics and moraine-dammed lakes all need a former glacier, meaning a cold-stage snowline below the island's summits. With peaks of a few hundred metres, that means high latitude (Scotland, Faroes). Unverified for any specific latitude.

## 7. Real islands as models

- **Staffa (about 0.3 km²).** A single flow: a tuff base, a lower colonnade forming sea cliffs and Fingal's Cave, and a chaotic entablature cap. The sea cave follows the column joints ([Phillips et al.](https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/output/1470309)). Lesson: one well-structured lava unit can make a whole island's character.
- **Trotternish, Skye (a peninsula about 30 km long).** A basalt lava pile over weak Jurassic sediments, with a dolerite sill at the slide base. The result is a continuous east-facing scarp, rotated blocks, pinnacles and closed hollows ([Scottish Geology Trust](https://geoguide.scottishgeologytrust.org/page/2842)). Lesson: caprock plus a weak layer plus dip gives cuesta, scarp and landslide complex together.
- **Arran (about 30 km).** A fault splits a granite north (peaks, tors, glacial corries) from a sedimentary south. Dike swarms make coastal ridges, and sills make ledges ([Arran Museum](https://www.arranmuseum.co.uk/what-will-you-see/geology/the-geological-history); [Bath Geol. Soc.](https://bathgeolsoc.org.uk/journal/articles/2012/2012_Trio_Scotland.pdf)). Lesson: two rock domains split by one fault double an island's variety.
- **Lord Howe (about 10 km).** The remnant of a roughly 30 km, 8°-sloped shield. Caldera-fill basalt stands as the 875 m southern mountains, a wave-cut shelf about 15 km wide surrounds the island, and a plug forms an offshore stack ([LHI Museum](https://lhimuseum.com/learn/geology/)). Lesson: most of the original cone has been eroded away; resistant infill and plugs remain. (The 875 m height is my recollection and unverified.)
- **Yakushima (about 25–30 km).** A rising granite pluton rimmed by hornfels-hardened sediments, with corestone boulders and steep falls ([MLIT](https://www.mlit.go.jp/tagengo-db/common/001549548.docx)). Lesson: one massive, widely jointed body gives tors, boulder rivers and slabby falls.
- *Kauaʻi Na Pali (not a small island)* has wave-cut basalt cliffs up to about 1,200 m and amphitheatre-headed valleys. Valleys cut back from the coast keep walls as steep as the sea cliffs ([AGU 2015](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015AGUFMEP53B1027O)).

## What could not be verified

Typical waterfall height by stream size; fin width; fault-scarp sizes; typical cave-passage dimensions and cave-entrance densities by terrain type; exfoliation sheet thickness; Devils Postpile and Staffa column diameters; Lord Howe's peak height; Manjanggul dimensions and Jeju's cone count; the original Costa & Schuster one-year survival figure (85% failed within a year, per a [secondary review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/earth-science/articles/10.3389/feart.2022.981068/pdf)); hoodoos and earth pillars in maritime climates; and the depositional coast (spits, tombolos, dunes, tidal flats), which was not researched.

## Host rules (my inference from the sources above)

Scale key: **V** needs 3D voxels; **H** fits a heightfield; **S** the 16 m simulation must know about it (it shapes drainage or relief at the cell scale).

| Landform | Conditions to test | Typical size | Scale |
|---|---|---|---|
| Structural bench / stepped valley side | Flat-lying alternating strong and weak units; valley cuts through them | Step height = unit thickness (~10–30 m) | S + H |
| Cuesta scarp and dip slope | Resistant unit dipping <20°; erosion exposes the up-dip edge | Scarp = unit thickness; km length | S |
| Hogback / flatiron | Resistant unit dipping steeply; cross-valleys cut it | Ridge tens of m high | S + H |

| Slot | As gorge, plus small catchment and vertical joints | 1–5 m wide | V |
